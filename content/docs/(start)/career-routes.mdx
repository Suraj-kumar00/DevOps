There is no single way into DevOps, platform, cloud or SRE work, and what you already know changes where you should start. Find your route below, then follow the [90-day path](/docs/90-day-path) from the phase that fits you.

## Pick your route

<Tabs items={['No tech background', 'IT support or sysadmin', 'Software developer', 'Student or graduate']}>
  <Tab value="No tech background">
    **You have:** a career in another field and strong habits such as communication, project work or attention to detail.

    **Start with:** Phase 1 of the [90-day path](/docs/90-day-path), from the very first Linux command. Do not skip it; it is the base of everything else.

    **Realistic first target:** roles with "cloud support", "operations" or "junior cloud engineer" in the title, and technical support roles at cloud and software companies. This is our advice based on how roles are described, not a guarantee. Many people reach DevOps through a first role near it.

    **Your advantage:** you can explain technical work to non-technical people, which many engineers cannot.
  </Tab>
  <Tab value="IT support or sysadmin">
    **You have:** hands-on Linux or Windows, networking and troubleshooting, and the habit of keeping systems up.

    **Start with:** skim Phase 1, then spend your time on Phase 2: Git, containers, CI/CD and infrastructure as code. The big change in this route is that you stop fixing machines by hand and start describing them in code.

    **Realistic first target:** junior DevOps, cloud or site reliability roles in your own company or in a similar one.

    **Your advantage:** you know what breaks. That is exactly what Project 3 is about.
  </Tab>
  <Tab value="Software developer">
    **You have:** programming and Git, and probably some experience deploying code that someone else runs.

    **Start with:** Phase 2, with extra attention to Linux and networking, which developers often skip. Make Project 2 fully automated and Project 3 about failure.

    **Realistic first target:** a platform or DevOps role in your current company, which is often the easiest transition because they already trust you.

    **Your advantage:** you understand what developers need from a platform.
  </Tab>
  <Tab value="Student or graduate">
    **You have:** theory and time, and probably no experience of running anything.

    **Start with:** Phase 1 in full, and begin Project 1 in the first month. Your degree shows you can study. The projects show that you can build.

    **Realistic first target:** graduate programmes, internships and junior roles in operations, cloud or software engineering with an operations component.

    **Your advantage:** time to build three real projects before you apply.
  </Tab>
</Tabs>

## Be realistic about the market

DevOps is often described as an advanced role. Mike Pfeiffer, writing on DEV Community about [starting a DevOps career from scratch](https://dev.to/cloudskills/starting-your-devops-career-from-scratch-2495), advises that people starting from zero aim first for an entry-level cloud engineering role. Opensource.com's guide to [transitioning into DevOps](https://opensource.com/article/19/7/how-transition-career-devops-engineer) starts with Linux and a scripting language.

When we searched job-board listings in November 2026, several posts labelled "junior" or "entry level" still asked for hands-on Kubernetes, Terraform or CI/CD experience, and at least one was titled a junior-to-mid level role. This was a small, informal look, not a study. A [dev.to post](https://dev.to/dev_tips/junior-dev-roles-are-rigged-the-entry-level-jobs-secretly-want-seniors-and-listings-are-2n22) describes the same experience paradox for software roles more widely.

What this means for you:

- **Apply with proof.** Your projects stand in for the experience the listing asks for.
- **Apply widely.** A title on a job post is not a strict requirement. Read what the role does.
- **Accept a stepping stone.** A cloud support or operations role that gets you real production experience is a good outcome, not a failure.

## If you are in India

The path is the same and the content is global, but a few things differ:

- **Pay figures vary a lot between sources, so treat any single number with care.** Careers360 reports an average fresher DevOps salary of Rs. 400,907 a year, and its table rises with experience in its [DevOps salary guide](https://www.careers360.com/careers/articles/devops-engineer-salary-in-india). Other guides, including one from [Scaler](https://www.scaler.com/blog/devops-engineer-salary/), publish different ranges. We do not quote them here because we could not check their methods. Use several current sources and ask people at the companies you target.
- **Check where the roles are before you decide to move.** Blog posts claim that a large share of DevOps listings are in a few cities, but we could not verify a figure. Search job boards for the city and remote roles yourself.
- **A portfolio matters more when you have no experience to show.** Projects 1 and 2 map to the kind of work a junior does first: automating routine tasks and supporting delivery pipelines.

<Callout type="warn" title="How we treat numbers">
  Salary and demand figures change quickly and come mostly from blogs and course providers, who have a reason to make a career look attractive. We link the source, and we say when we cannot verify a figure. If you find an official or better dataset, please [tell us](/docs/contributing).
</Callout>

## Certifications

Certifications can support a portfolio but the guides we read do not treat them as a replacement. The AWS Builder article above says hiring managers want to see whether someone can ship, automate and fix things when they break. Take a certificate if your target employer asks for it or if it gives you a structured way to learn a cloud provider, and still build the projects.
